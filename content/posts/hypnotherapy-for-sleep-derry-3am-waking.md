## Why Your Brain Wakes You at the Same Time Every Night

Sleep is not a switch. It is a state your nervous system has to feel safe enough to enter, and safe enough to stay in. When you have had a run of bad nights, your brain starts to associate the bed itself with the frustration of lying awake. Over time, that association becomes automatic. You do not choose to wake at 3am. Your nervous system has been trained to expect it, the same way it might learn to flinch at a loud noise.

This is what sleep specialists call conditioned arousal. Your body slips into a low-grade version of fight-or-flight right around the time you would normally be in deep sleep. Your heart rate ticks up slightly, your thoughts speed up, and full sleep feels a step out of reach even though you are exhausted. Sleep hygiene advice, like avoiding screens or cutting caffeine, addresses the surroundings. It does little for a nervous system that has already learned to treat the bed as a place to stay alert.

This is also why so many people who wake at 3am describe feeling fine in the day and then dreading bedtime by evening. The dread itself becomes part of the pattern. Your mind starts anticipating the wake-up before it happens, which reinforces the exact response you are trying to break.

## What the Research Says About Hypnotherapy and Sleep

The NHS lists Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia, known as CBT-I, as the first treatment to try for ongoing sleep problems, because clinical trials have shown it helps most people who try it. Hypnotherapy works from a related starting point: both approaches aim to interrupt the thoughts and physical responses that keep insomnia going, rather than masking the symptom for one night.

A 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis looked specifically at hypnosis-based approaches for insomnia across a set of randomised controlled trials, and a further meta-analysis in 2018 reported improvements in some sleep outcomes among people who used hypnosis. A separate narrative review of the literature noted that sleep research has recorded an increase in deep sleep waves among people in a hypnotic state, which lines up with what many clients describe: a session that leaves them properly rested rather than just relaxed for an hour.

It is worth being straight with you about where the evidence currently stands. Several reviewers have flagged that the research base is smaller and more mixed in quality than the evidence behind CBT-I, and some studies lack the sample size to draw firm conclusions. Hypnotherapy is a genuinely promising, well-established complementary approach. It is not a guaranteed fix, and nobody offering you one is being straight with you. What clients commonly report at Derry Hypnosis is a calmer nervous system at bedtime, fewer racing thoughts, and a gradual return to sleeping through. Results vary from person to person, and if your insomnia is chronic or linked to a diagnosed condition, your GP should be part of the plan alongside any hypnotherapy sessions.
