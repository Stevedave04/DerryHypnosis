Almost everyone arrives at a first session with the same two questions, and most are slightly embarrassed to ask them.

*Will I be unconscious?* And *will I say something I would rather keep to myself?*

The answer to both is no. But that deserves more than a one-word reassurance, because the fear behind the question is reasonable. Nearly everything most people know about hypnosis comes from stage shows and films, and stage hypnosis is entertainment built from volunteers who want to perform. It has about as much to do with clinical hypnotherapy as a magic trick has to do with surgery.

So here is the ordinary, unglamorous truth about what actually happens.

## Key Takeaways

- Hypnosis is not sleep and not unconsciousness. You are awake, aware, and can hear everything in the room.
- You stay in control throughout. You cannot be made to do or say anything against your values, and you can open your eyes and stop at any point.
- Most people describe it as similar to being absorbed in a film, or the drowsy few minutes before sleep — familiar rather than strange.
- The most common reaction afterwards is mild disappointment that it felt so normal, followed by surprise when things start changing.

## You Have Already Been in This State, Many Times

Hypnosis is not something done to you from outside. It is a naturally occurring state of focused attention that you enter several times a day without noticing.

You have driven a familiar route and arrived with no memory of the last three miles. You have been so deep in a book that someone had to say your name twice. You have watched a film and felt genuine grief for a person who does not exist. You were not unconscious in any of those moments. You were absorbed — attention narrowed onto one thing, the background filtered out.

That is the state. The only difference in a clinical session is that the absorption is deliberate, and it is being used for something you actually want.

This is also why "I don't think I can be hypnotised" is rarely true. If you can become engrossed in something, you have the capacity. What varies is depth, and the useful news is that depth is not the point. Light trance is enough for most therapeutic work.

## What the Hour Actually Looks Like

There is no swinging watch. There is a comfortable chair, a quiet room and a conversation.

**The first part is talking.** Most of a first appointment is spent with your eyes wide open, working out what is actually going on. What you want to change, what you have already tried, when the pattern started, what triggers it. This is not therapy in the sense of dredging through your past — it is working out precisely where to aim. The session that follows is built on what you say here, which is why no two are identical.

**Then you settle.** You sit back, usually close your eyes, and follow the sound of Tracey's voice. There is no moment where you go under. There is no line you cross. It is gradual, and it feels like relaxing, because that is what it is.

**Then the work.** In that settled state, the critical, arguing part of your mind quietens down. This is the part that hears "you feel calm about flying" and immediately answers "no I don't, I hate flying". When it is not interrupting, new ideas get a fair hearing for once. That is the whole mechanism. Not control — access.

**Then you come back.** You are brought fully alert, wide awake and grounded. Most people stretch, have a glass of water, and drive home perfectly normally.

## So What Does It Feel Like?

The honest answer is: pleasant, and much less dramatic than you are expecting.

Most people report a heavy, warm, comfortable feeling, the sort you get when you are properly relaxed on a sofa. Hands and feet often feel heavier. Time gets strange — a forty-minute session regularly feels like ten. Some people feel a floating sensation. Some feel nothing unusual at all beyond being very relaxed.

Throughout it, you hear everything. Traffic outside, a door somewhere in the building, every word that is said. Most people are mildly annoyed by this at first, because they were expecting oblivion and instead they are just sitting there listening, fully aware, thinking *is this working?*

It is working. That is what it feels like.

The most common thing people say when they open their eyes is some version of "I don't think I went under". They are often the same people who come back a fortnight later reporting that they have not thought about cigarettes once.

## The Control Question, Answered Properly

You cannot be made to do anything that conflicts with your values.

Hypnosis does not override your judgement, your morals or your will. The part of you that decides what is acceptable does not switch off. If a suggestion is given that you genuinely disagree with, you simply will not act on it — your mind rejects it the way it rejects any bad idea.

You can also stop whenever you like. You can open your eyes, speak, shift in the chair, ask a question, or end the session. People sometimes worry about being stuck in hypnosis. It does not happen. If left entirely alone you would either drift into ordinary sleep and wake up normally, or simply open your eyes when you got bored.

Nor will you blurt out secrets. You are not interrogated, and you are not compelled to answer anything. You share exactly what you choose to share, the same as any other conversation.

The stage show is a different thing entirely. Those volunteers walked onto a stage in front of an audience knowing what was expected of them. They were not stripped of choice; they had already chosen.

## Who It Does Not Suit

Three honest limits, because the point of this article is a straight answer rather than a sales pitch.

It requires you to genuinely want the change. Hypnotherapy is not effective on somebody dragged along by a spouse who wants them to stop smoking. The motivation has to be yours.

It is not a substitute for medical or psychiatric care. Certain conditions need a doctor, and some need specialist mental health services. A responsible hypnotherapist will tell you when you are in the wrong room, and where the right one is.

And it is not magic. It is a method. It works with you, not on you, and there is usually something to practise between sessions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will I be asleep or unconscious during hypnosis?

No. Hypnosis is a state of focused attention, not sleep or sedation. You remain awake and aware throughout, can hear everything in the room, and will remember the session afterwards.

### Can I be made to do something against my will?

No. Your values and judgement remain fully intact under hypnosis. You cannot be made to act against them, you can end the session at any time, and you will not reveal anything you choose to keep private.

### What if I have an analytical mind that will not switch off?

Analytical people do well with hypnotherapy. You do not need to stop thinking for it to work, and specific techniques are used to work with a busy, questioning mind rather than against it.

### Can everyone be hypnotised?

Most people can, provided they are willing and feel safe. It is a natural state everyone experiences daily. Depth varies between people, but even a light trance is effective for therapeutic work.

### Is it safe to drive afterwards?

Yes. A re-alerting process at the end of every session leaves you fully awake and grounded. You will feel calm but entirely capable of driving and getting on with your day.

### How will I know it worked if it just felt like relaxing?

You judge it by what changes, not by how unusual the session felt. Most people notice differences in the days and weeks afterwards — a craving that does not arrive, a situation that no longer triggers the old response — rather than during the session itself.

### Is hypnotherapy safe for children and teenagers?

Yes. Young people often respond very naturally to it because of their imagination. Tracey works with teenagers on exam stress, confidence and habits, and is fully accredited and vetted.
