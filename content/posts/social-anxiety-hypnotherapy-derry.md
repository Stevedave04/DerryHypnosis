## More Than Shyness

Almost everyone feels nervous in social situations sometimes. A first date. A job interview. Speaking to a room of strangers. This is normal, and it passes.

Social anxiety is different. It is a persistent, intense fear of being watched, judged, embarrassed, or rejected — one that arises in a wide range of ordinary situations that most people navigate without a second thought.

Attending a party. Making a phone call. Eating in a restaurant. Joining a conversation already in progress. Walking into a room where people might look up.

For people with social anxiety, these are not minor inconveniences. They are genuinely threatening situations that trigger physical symptoms — racing heart, flushed face, trembling hands, nausea — and a desperate urge to escape or avoid.

Over time, avoidance becomes a way of life. And as the avoided situations multiply, the world gets smaller.

## What Is Actually Happening

Social anxiety, like all anxiety, is a threat response that has been misfired. The brain has — at some point, often in adolescence or early adulthood — learned to categorise social evaluation as *dangerous*.

This usually has roots in experience. Humiliation in front of peers. Criticism from a parent who equated approval with love. A defining moment of social failure that the subconscious locked onto and generalised into a rule: *other people's judgment = threat to survival*.

The logical mind may know this is disproportionate. But social anxiety doesn't live in the logical mind. It lives in the amygdala — the brain's threat-detection centre — which operates much faster than conscious thought and doesn't respond well to rational argument.

This is why telling yourself "it'll be fine" rarely helps. You're talking to the wrong part of the brain.

## The Avoidance Trap

One of the most insidious features of social anxiety is that avoidance makes it worse.

When you avoid a situation that feels threatening, you get short-term relief. The anxiety drops. The brain records this as confirmation that the threat was real — *I escaped danger* — and the feared situation becomes more frightening, not less.

Each time you cancel plans, leave a social situation early, or rehearse conversation scripts to avoid spontaneity, the avoidance pattern is reinforced and the anxiety grows.

Breaking this cycle through conscious effort alone — forcing yourself into feared situations before the underlying threat association has been addressed — is both exhausting and often counterproductive. The repeated experience of feeling terrified while trying to appear normal is not healing. It is just suffering.

Lasting change requires updating the threat association at its source, in the subconscious, where it was written.

## How Hypnotherapy Addresses Social Anxiety

Clinical hypnotherapy offers a direct route to the subconscious patterns maintaining the anxiety.

In a deeply relaxed hypnotic state, the critical, defensive part of the conscious mind quietens — not because you have been put to sleep or made vulnerable, but because the brain naturally shifts into a mode where new learning is possible without the usual resistance.

In this state, Tracey works to:

**Locate the origin.** Often social anxiety can be traced to one or several defining experiences that the subconscious generalised into a global rule about social danger. Identifying and updating these memories — reducing the emotional charge they carry — is frequently the most powerful part of the work.

**Reprogram the threat response.** The amygdala's sensitivity to social situations can be recalibrated. Rather than scanning for threat in every social environment, the brain can learn to default to a state of ease, curiosity, and openness.

**Build a new social identity.** Many people with social anxiety have spent years thinking of themselves as "socially awkward," "boring," or "unlikeable." These beliefs were installed by painful experience and maintained by avoidance — and they are not accurate. Hypnotherapy helps replace them with a more realistic and compassionate self-image.

**Create an embodied sense of ease.** Through visualisation and suggestion, the client begins to experience social situations as comfortable, even enjoyable, at a felt, bodily level — before they have had to do anything in the real world. This pre-wires new neural pathways, making the real-world experience significantly less threatening when it comes.

## What Clients Typically Experience

The shift is rarely sudden, but it is often surprisingly quick. Within a few sessions, most clients notice:

- Less anticipatory anxiety before social situations they previously dreaded
- Reduced physical symptoms (blushing, shaking, nausea) in social settings
- Greater ease entering conversations and being spontaneous
- The ability to stop mid-sentence without catastrophising
- Less mental rehearsal and post-event analysis ("What did they think of me?")
- A growing sense of themselves as someone who is welcome and at ease in the world

For some, changes are more dramatic — the sudden absence of an anxiety that had been present for so long they had mistaken it for personality.
