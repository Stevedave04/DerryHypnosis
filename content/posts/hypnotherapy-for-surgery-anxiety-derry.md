You have the date. It is written on the calendar, and every time you look at it your stomach tightens.

Maybe it is a knee replacement, a gallbladder, a hernia, something bigger. Maybe you have been waiting eighteen months and the letter finally came. Either way, the operation itself is only part of what you are carrying. There is the night before. The walk into the building. The cannula. The mask. The not knowing exactly what happens once you are asleep, and whether you will feel it afterwards.

Most people are told to try not to worry about it. That is not advice, it is a wish.

## Why the Waiting Is Often the Worst Part

Ask anyone who has had an operation what the hardest bit was. A surprising number will not say the surgery. They will say the weeks before it.

There is a reason for that. Your nervous system cannot tell the difference between a threat that is happening and a threat you are vividly imagining. When you lie awake running through the procedure, picturing the theatre, imagining what could go wrong, your body responds as though it is happening now. Heart rate up. Muscles tight. Sleep broken. Then you do it again the next night, and the night after.

By the time surgery day arrives, some people have rehearsed the worst version of it several hundred times. They walk in already exhausted.

This is not irrational. Surgery genuinely is a significant event, and a degree of apprehension is sensible. The problem is not that you feel something. The problem is that the feeling runs unchecked for weeks, on a loop, with nowhere to go.

## What Hypnotherapy Actually Works On

Clinical hypnotherapy does not pretend the operation is not happening, and it does not talk you out of your concerns. It works at the level where the anticipation is being generated.

In a relaxed, focused state, we can:

- **Interrupt the rehearsal loop.** The mental film of everything going wrong is a habit, and habits can be changed. We replace it with a calmer, more realistic rehearsal of the day.
- **Address the specific fear.** For most people it is not "surgery" in the abstract. It is needles. Or the anaesthetic mask. Or being out of control while unconscious. Or hospitals themselves, often because of something that happened to someone else years ago. Naming the actual fear makes it workable.
- **Build trust in the people treating you.** Feeling handed over to strangers is its own stress. Much of this work is about arriving able to let your team do their job.
- **Give you something to use on the day.** A simple anchor or breathing pattern you have practised in advance, so that when you are sitting in a gown waiting to be called, you have somewhere to put your attention.

The aim is not to feel nothing. It is to arrive steady, rested, and able to cope with the day as it actually unfolds.

## The Part Nobody Prepares You For: Afterwards

Recovery is where a lot of people come unstuck, and almost nobody warns them.

You are sent home and told to rest. But rest is not a switch. You are sore, you are bored, you cannot do the things you normally do, and you are watching the house fall behind around you. For someone used to being capable and busy, being temporarily dependent is genuinely difficult. Frustration builds. Sleep is broken by discomfort. Progress feels slower than you expected, and you start to wonder whether it is going wrong.

None of that is a complication. It is a normal emotional response to an abnormal few weeks — and it is a lot easier with support.

Hypnotherapy during recovery focuses on settling the nervous system so rest is actually restful, managing the frustration of a slow week, and keeping your attention on how far you have come rather than how far there is to go. For many people that is the difference between enduring recovery and moving through it.

## Where This Fits With Your Medical Care

This matters, so it is worth being direct.

Clinical hypnotherapy is a complementary approach. It works with anxiety, comfort and mindset. It is not a treatment for your medical condition, it is not a substitute for anaesthetic or pain relief, and it does not change anything about your surgical plan.

Tell your surgical team and your GP what you are doing. Keep taking what you have been prescribed. Follow the post-operative instructions you are given, not something you read online. If you have concerns about the procedure itself, those belong with your consultant, who can answer them properly.

What hypnotherapy offers is the psychological side of the experience, which is the part of surgery most likely to be left entirely to you to manage alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can hypnotherapy help if I am frightened of needles rather than the surgery itself?

Yes, and this is more common than people expect. Needle fear is a specific phobic response and often responds well to focused work. It is worth raising early, because it affects blood tests and cannulas as much as the operation, and those come first.

### How close to my operation can I book?

There is no hard cut-off. Working further in advance gives more room, but short-notice sessions are often still useful — one client described being seen at short notice and going in calm. Mention your date when you make contact so it can be prioritised.

### Will hypnotherapy reduce the pain after my operation?

Hypnotherapy works with how settled and comfortable you feel, and with the anxiety that tends to make discomfort harder to bear. It is not pain relief and it does not replace what you are prescribed. Keep taking your medication as directed and speak to your medical team about pain management.

### Is this suitable if I am having a procedure under local anaesthetic while awake?

Often yes. Being awake during a procedure brings its own anxieties, and being able to settle yourself and keep your attention somewhere useful can make a real difference. Let Tracey know the details when you book.

### Do I need to tell my surgeon or GP?

Yes, please do. Hypnotherapy is complementary to your medical care, not separate from it, and your team should have the full picture. It is not something to hide, and no reasonable clinician will object to you managing your anxiety.
