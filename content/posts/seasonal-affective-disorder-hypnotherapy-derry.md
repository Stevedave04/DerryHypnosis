## What SAD Actually Is

Seasonal Affective Disorder is depression that follows a seasonal pattern, most commonly starting in autumn and lifting in spring. It is recognised clinically. It is not the same as generally preferring summer.

The symptoms are the symptoms of depression, with some features that show up more often in the seasonal form:

- Persistent low mood through the darker months
- Losing interest in things you normally enjoy
- Low energy and sleeping much more than usual, yet still feeling unrefreshed
- Craving carbohydrates and sugar, often with weight gain over winter
- Difficulty concentrating
- Feeling irritable, tearful, or wanting to withdraw from people
- A sense of dread as the nights start drawing in, sometimes from as early as August

The current understanding is that reduced daylight disrupts the body clock and affects melatonin and serotonin, though the full mechanism is still being researched. What matters practically is that it follows the light, which is why where you live makes a genuine difference.

## Why Winter Here Is Harder Than Most People Admit

Derry sits at about 55 degrees north. That is further north than Moscow.

In late December we get somewhere around seven hours between sunrise and sunset, against roughly seventeen at midsummer. And this is the North West, so a decent share of those seven hours is grey. If you work indoors, it is entirely possible to go days in winter without being outside in meaningful daylight at all.

People often blame themselves for struggling in January. It is worth sitting with the fact that you are doing it on a fraction of the light your body evolved to expect.

## Start With Your GP. Genuinely.

This is the part where a hypnotherapy website is supposed to pivot to the sales pitch. It should not, and here is why.

SAD is depression. Depression is a medical condition that responds to established treatments, and those treatments have a far stronger evidence base than anything I do. If your mood drops every winter to the point it affects your work, your relationships or your ability to get through the day, that is a conversation for your GP.

What they may discuss with you:

**Light therapy.** A light box of a specified intensity, used for a set period each morning. This is the treatment most specifically associated with SAD.

**Talking therapy.** CBT in particular, sometimes adapted for seasonal patterns. NICE recommends talking therapy for depression, and there is evidence for CBT specifically in SAD.

**Antidepressant medication.** For some people, sometimes started before the season begins rather than after symptoms arrive.

**Practical changes.** Getting outside in the morning, exercise, and managing the ordinary winter pressures that compound it.

None of that is a reason to avoid hypnotherapy. It is a reason to get the diagnosis and the main treatment right first, rather than instead.

## So Where Does Hypnotherapy Actually Fit?

Honestly: at the edges. But the edges matter more than they sound.

There is no strong body of research on hypnotherapy for Seasonal Affective Disorder specifically. Anyone telling you it treats or cures SAD is overselling. What hypnotherapy works with are the patterns that grow around the low mood and make the whole winter heavier than it needs to be.

**The sleep that has gone wrong.** SAD frequently brings oversleeping that leaves you more tired, or broken sleep that never restores you. Hypnotherapy works with the wind-down and the racing mind at night. This is the piece with the clearest overlap.

**The eating that took over.** The carbohydrate craving is a real feature of the condition, not weakness. But the habit loop that forms around it — the biscuits at nine, the "the week is ruined anyway" — is exactly the kind of pattern this work addresses.

**The dread.** For a lot of people the anticipation starts in August, and the months of bracing for it cost almost as much as the winter itself. That anticipatory anxiety is very workable.

**The self-talk.** The voice saying you are lazy, that everyone else is coping, that you should have snapped out of this. It is not true, it makes everything worse, and it is not fixed by daylight.

**The withdrawal.** Cancelling plans feels like relief and then deepens the isolation. Rebuilding the willingness to show up matters.

What that adds up to is not a cure. It is carrying less. If the condition is being treated properly and the habits stacked on top of it are also being addressed, winter tends to be more manageable than treating either alone.

## What This Would Look Like in Practice

If you came in about winter mood, the first appointment would be mostly talking. Specifically: what has your GP said, are you being treated for anything currently, and what actually happens to you between October and March.

If it sounded like undiagnosed depression, you would be encouraged to see your GP, and told plainly that hypnotherapy is not a substitute for that. That is not a formality. Referring on when something sits outside your scope is part of practising responsibly.

Where it goes from there depends on which piece is costing you most. For many people it is sleep, because everything else is harder on four broken hours. For others it is the eating, or the dread, or the inner critic. It is usually a handful of sessions rather than an open-ended commitment, and there is generally something to practise between them.

The timing worth knowing: starting in September or October, before the worst of it, tends to be more useful than starting in February when you are already worn down. The same logic as beginning light therapy early.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can hypnotherapy cure Seasonal Affective Disorder?

No. SAD is a form of depression and should be assessed and treated by your GP, who may discuss light therapy, talking therapy such as CBT, or medication. There is no strong evidence base for hypnotherapy as a treatment for SAD specifically. It may help with associated patterns such as disrupted sleep, comfort eating, anticipatory dread and negative self-talk, alongside proper medical care.

### What is the difference between SAD and the winter blues?

Many people feel a bit flatter in winter. SAD is more than that: a persistent low mood with a clear seasonal pattern, significant enough to affect daily functioning, often with oversleeping, carbohydrate craving and withdrawal. If it is interfering with work, relationships or daily life, it is worth a GP appointment rather than waiting it out.

### Is SAD worse in Northern Ireland because of the latitude?

Derry sits at roughly 55 degrees north, giving around seven hours of daylight in late December against about seventeen in June, frequently under heavy cloud. Reduced daylight is central to SAD, so northern latitudes and overcast climates are a recognised factor.

### Should I see my GP or a hypnotherapist first?

Your GP, if you suspect SAD or any depression. Get the assessment and the main treatment in place first. Hypnotherapy works best as support alongside that, not as a replacement for it.

### When is the best time to start working on winter mood?

Before it peaks. September and October are more useful than January or February, when you are already depleted. Preventative approaches are common with SAD, which is why some treatments are started ahead of the season.

### Can hypnotherapy help with winter weight gain and carbohydrate cravings?

It can help with the habit patterns around eating — the evening routine, the emotional triggers, the all-or-nothing thinking. It does not override the biological drive that comes with the condition, which is one more reason to have the underlying low mood properly assessed.
