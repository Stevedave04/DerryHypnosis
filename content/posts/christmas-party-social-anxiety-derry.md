The email goes round in October. Someone starts a spreadsheet for the food order. And a small cold weight settles in your stomach that will not fully lift until the night is over.

You will probably go. You will have two drinks faster than you meant to, spend a chunk of the evening doing something useful with your hands near the bar, and leave as early as is socially survivable. Then you will lie awake replaying one thing you said.

Nobody at that table knows any of this is happening.

## Key Takeaways

- The work Christmas party is uniquely difficult: colleagues, alcohol, no clear role, no defined end time, and consequences on Monday morning.
- Most of the cost is not the night itself. It is the weeks of anticipation beforehand and the replaying afterwards.
- Alcohol feels like the solution and reliably makes the replaying worse.
- Social anxiety responds well to clinical work, and October is the right time to start if the date is in December.

## Why This One Is Harder Than an Ordinary Night Out

People who manage fine in most situations often find the work do uniquely awful, and there are specific reasons.

**There is no role.** At work you know what you are for. You have a job, a function, things to talk about. Strip that away, put everyone in different clothes in a bar, and the structure that normally carries you is gone.

**The audience matters on Monday.** A stranger in a pub is a stranger. These people decide what you are like. Anything awkward follows you back to the desk and potentially into next year.

**Nobody tells you when it ends.** Leaving too early gets noticed. Staying too long risks the bit of the night you would rather avoid. So you spend the evening running a calculation instead of being in it.

**Alcohol is the structure.** The drink is doing the job your role normally does, which is why it goes down faster than you planned.

**Hierarchy is in the room.** Your manager is there. Someone always says something unwise to someone senior. Part of you spends the night making sure it is not you.

## The Dread Costs More Than the Night

Here is the thing people underestimate. If the party is on the 12th of December and the invite lands on the 10th of October, you do not pay for one evening. You pay for nine weeks.

Nine weeks of it surfacing when you are trying to sleep. Nine weeks of rehearsing conversations that will not happen. Then a few days afterwards of the post-mortem, where you examine one sentence you said at the bar from every possible angle and conclude that everyone noticed.

They did not. They were busy running their own version of the same calculation.

That anticipation-and-replay cycle is usually the larger part of the problem, and it is also the most workable part.

## Things That Make It Worse

**Drinking to get through it.** It works for about an hour, then it loosens the judgement you were relying on, and it reliably intensifies the next-day replaying. The thing that made the night bearable is the thing that makes Saturday unbearable.

**Rehearsing conversations.** Mentally running through what you will say feels like preparation. It is actually practising the anxiety, and the conversation never goes the way you scripted.

**Deciding in advance it will be awful.** You will find evidence for whatever you expect.

**Not going.** The relief when you cancel is immediate and genuine, and it is also the thing that makes next year harder. Avoidance always charges interest.

## What Actually Helps

A few practical things, and they are not groundbreaking:

- **Arrive early, not late.** Walking into a room of six people is easier than walking into a room of sixty. You get to be part of the group that formed, rather than joining one.
- **Have a job.** Get the drinks in, sort the music, help with the food. A role is the thing you are missing, so take one.
- **Decide your exit before you go.** "I am leaving at half eleven" removes the running calculation. Tell one person so it is a plan rather than a decision.
- **Pace the first hour.** The first drink is doing work. The third is borrowing from tomorrow.
- **Have two questions ready.** Not scripts — questions. People will happily talk about Christmas plans and what they are doing between the days.

These help at the margins. They do not touch the dread, which is the bigger problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is it social anxiety or am I just shy?

Shyness is a temperament. Social anxiety is when the fear of being judged is intense enough to affect what you do: avoiding events, dreading them for weeks, or needing alcohol to manage them. If it is costing you opportunities or sleep, the label matters less than the fact that it responds to work.

### Should I just skip the Christmas party?

That is your call, and one night is not a moral test. But if the pattern is that you avoid every social work event, avoidance tends to narrow things over time and makes the next invitation harder. Skipping one party is fine; building a life around skipping is the thing to address.

### Can hypnotherapy help before a party in a few weeks' time?

Often yes, though more time is better. Social anxiety work typically takes a handful of sessions, so starting in October or early November for a December date is more comfortable than booking the week before.

### Will hypnotherapy make me outgoing?

No, and it should not try to. The aim is not turning an introvert into the loudest person at the table. It is removing the dread so you can attend as yourself, stay as long as you actually want to, and leave without a weekend of replaying.

### What if my anxiety is mostly about speaking in front of colleagues?

That overlaps heavily with public speaking work — the toast, the speech, the thing where everyone turns to look at you. Same underlying mechanism, slightly different focus. Mention it when you get in touch.

### I only feel able to socialise after a few drinks. Is that a problem?

It is worth examining. Using alcohol to manage anxiety is extremely common and tends to deepen the underlying problem while masking it. No judgement attached, but it is a useful thing to raise.
