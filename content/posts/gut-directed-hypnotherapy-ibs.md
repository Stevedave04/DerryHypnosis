## Living With IBS Is Exhausting

If you have irritable bowel syndrome, you already know the routine. The unpredictable mornings. The meals you avoid. The mental map of every public toilet within reach. The anxiety that builds before any event, not because you're worried about the event itself, but because your gut has become unreliable in a way that feels deeply humiliating.

IBS affects roughly one in five people in the UK. It is the most commonly diagnosed gastrointestinal condition, yet many people with IBS spend years being told there is nothing structurally wrong, offered dietary advice that helps partially at best, or prescribed medication that manages symptoms without addressing what is actually going on.

What is actually going on involves your brain far more than most people realise.

## The Gut-Brain Connection

The gut contains more than 100 million nerve cells. It communicates with the brain constantly via the vagus nerve, and the relationship runs in both directions: the brain influences the gut, and the gut influences the brain. This bidirectional system is called the gut-brain axis.

In IBS, something in this communication has gone wrong. Research consistently shows that people with IBS have heightened sensitivity in the gut: the sensory threshold for pain is lower, and normal gut contractions that most people would not notice are experienced as cramping or urgency.

This heightened sensitivity is not imaginary and it is not a sign that someone is weak or anxious by nature. It is a change in how the nervous system processes gut signals, and it can be triggered by bacterial infections, periods of high stress, trauma, or simply a nervous system that has learned, over time, to treat the gut as a source of threat.

That learning happened below conscious awareness. Which is exactly why approaches that target the subconscious, including gut-directed hypnotherapy, have attracted serious clinical interest.

## What Gut-Directed Hypnotherapy Is

Gut-directed hypnotherapy is a specific form of clinical hypnotherapy developed in the 1980s by Professor Peter Whorwell at the University of Manchester. Unlike general relaxation or stress management, it uses hypnosis to address the gut directly: helping the nervous system recalibrate its response to gut signals, reducing hypersensitivity, and restoring a more normal relationship between the brain and the digestive system.

In a gut-directed hypnotherapy session, a client enters a deeply relaxed, focused state. The therapist then uses imagery and suggestion specifically targeted at the gut: warmth, calm, normal and comfortable movement. Over a course of sessions, these suggestions work to interrupt the learned pattern of hypersensitivity and replace it with a calmer, more regulated response.

It is not about belief. It is not about willpower. It works by accessing the same subconscious system that created the problem in the first place.

## What the Evidence Actually Shows

This is not fringe or unproven. Gut-directed hypnotherapy for IBS has been studied in randomised controlled trials since the 1980s, and the results are consistent.

In the original Manchester trials, Professor Whorwell found that 70 to 80 percent of IBS patients who had not responded to conventional treatment showed significant improvement after a course of gut-directed hypnotherapy. These improvements were maintained at follow-up periods of up to five years.

More recently, a 2021 systematic review published in the journal *Alimentary Pharmacology and Therapeutics* analysed multiple clinical trials and confirmed that gut-directed hypnotherapy produces significant improvements in overall IBS symptoms, abdominal pain, and quality of life compared to control conditions.

**NICE, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, recognised gut-directed hypnotherapy as a recommended treatment option for IBS in its guidelines** (CG61, updated 2017). The recommendation applies specifically to patients whose symptoms have not responded adequately to dietary changes and pharmacological treatments, which describes a very large proportion of people living with IBS.

That is a meaningful endorsement. NICE does not include treatments in its guidelines without robust evidence.

## A Note on Medical Advice

Gut-directed hypnotherapy is a complementary approach, not a replacement for medical investigation or treatment. If you have IBS symptoms you have not yet discussed with your GP, please do so. Conditions including inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, and other gastrointestinal conditions require medical diagnosis and management.

If you have already been diagnosed with IBS and have found that conventional treatments have not given you adequate relief, that is precisely the situation NICE guidance was written for, and that is where clinical hypnotherapy can make a genuine difference.

## Three Things You Can Do Right Now

If you are living with IBS and waiting to start working with a hypnotherapist, these practices will support your gut-brain axis in the meantime.

**Diaphragmatic breathing.** Slow, deep breathing activates the vagus nerve and signals safety to the nervous system. Even five minutes of slow, belly-led breathing before meals can reduce gut reactivity. Breathe in for four counts, out for six.

**Consistent meal timing.** The gut responds well to routine. Irregular eating times increase unpredictability in gut motility. Aim for meals at consistent times and avoid eating when you are rushed or stressed if possible.

**Reduce the fear of symptoms.** Anticipatory anxiety about IBS symptoms tends to amplify them. The moment you begin to worry whether your gut will behave, that worry signals threat to the nervous system, which makes symptoms more likely. Easier said than done, but this is one of the things gut-directed hypnotherapy addresses directly: it works to interrupt the anxiety-symptom loop, not just the symptoms themselves.
